## Why prices moved

**A rebound after a rough patch.** On Monday the S&P 500 jumped 2.4%, making up for a sell-off on 5–6 November, and the VIX, Wall Street’s fear gauge, fell 22% in a single day.[@glassdesk] Cheaper oil helped too: crude prices slipped for a second week as the US blockade of Iran eased fears of new fighting.[@reuters-oil]

explain: **Why does cheaper oil help shares?** Oil goes into transport, heating and making almost everything. When it gets cheaper, companies keep more of what they earn and households have more money left to spend.

**Prices rose faster than hoped, and interest rates followed.** US consumer prices (the CPI) rose 0.4% in October. Core prices rose 0.3%, the third month in a row at that pace, and the prices businesses charge each other (wholesale prices) were 6.5% higher than a year earlier.[@yardeni-week-ahead] Fed Governor Waller, one of the officials who vote on US interest rates at the Federal Reserve (the Fed), said a core reading near 0.3% could make a large December rate rise of 50 basis points possible. The 10-year yield climbed every day.[@glassdesk] By Thursday, traders were betting on a roughly 89% chance of a smaller 25 basis point rise in December, according to CME FedWatch, a tool that turns traders’ bets into odds.[@yahoo-zacks-nov13] That hit shares of smaller companies and property firms.[@investomine]

explain: **Inflation, the Fed and basis points.** Inflation is how fast prices rise. The CPI tracks the prices of a basket of things people buy; core inflation leaves out food and energy because their prices jump around a lot. To slow inflation down, the Fed, the US central bank, can raise interest rates. That makes borrowing more expensive and safe savings more rewarding, so shares look less attractive, especially for smaller companies and property firms that rely on borrowed money. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point.

**Nvidia lost its boss.** Nvidia’s board opened an investigation into Jensen Huang on 8 November, and he resigned on Thursday after reports of affairs outside his marriage. Finance chief Colette Kress is running the company for now.[@cnbc-huang-resigns] The stock fell as much as 14% during Thursday’s trading and ended that day down 4.3%. On Friday Nvidia’s market value dropped below $4 trillion for the first time since July 2025.[@cnbc-nvda-4t] Investors moved money to rival chipmakers: the bank Oppenheimer raised its rating on AMD, and Broadcom gained as big cloud companies build their own AI chips.[@statementdog]

**Europe shrugged off the chip sell-off.** The STOXX 600 rose 2.4% to its highest level ever, helped by strong bank profits and new orders for industrial companies.[@investomine] Insurers, oil companies and firms like Burberry and Munich Re made up for the falling chip stocks,[@mlq-europe] and investors put $35 billion into European stock funds in the week to 11 November.[@investomine] In Brussels, the BEL 20, an index full of steady “defensive” companies, set its 34th record close of the year on Friday, led by the bank KBC.[@lalibre-bel20]

explain: **Defensive shares** belong to companies that people need in good times and bad, such as energy and telecom providers, food makers or healthcare companies. Their prices usually swing less than those of fast-growing tech companies, so investors often turn to them when tech gets shaky.

**Crypto took the hit.** Bitcoin fell below $68,000 on Friday, under its August low and its weakest level since mid-August. Three things hit at once: bets on higher interest rates, forced selling (over $1.3 billion of bets closed automatically) and the turmoil at Nvidia.[@cryptoticker][@cryptodesk] Funds that hold bitcoin and trade like shares (spot bitcoin ETFs) had money flowing out for five days in a row, about $1.44 billion in total, up to 11 November.[@kucoin-daily]

explain: **Forced selling** happens when traders borrow money to bet that bitcoin will rise. If the price falls too far, the platform closes their bet automatically (it is “liquidated”), and that extra selling pushes the price down further. An **ETF** (exchange-traded fund) is a basket of investments that you can buy and sell on the stock market like a single share.

## Other news that mattered

- **US midterm elections change the picture.** Democrats won both chambers of Congress, and Elizabeth Warren is set to lead the Senate Banking Committee, which could change the path of US crypto rules in 2027.[@calebbrown] Analysts also expect possible new rules for data centres.[@seekingalpha-week-ahead]
- **Huang called before Congress.** Lawmakers want the former Nvidia boss to answer their questions in a hearing.[@cnn-huang-testify]
- **Alphabet pushes further into AI chips.** Google’s parent company announced a wider range of AI chips this week, setting itself up as a challenger to Nvidia.[@taipeitimes-amd-avgo]
- **The euro-area economy keeps growing.** GDP, the value of everything the economy produces, grew 0.4% from the previous quarter (1.3% from a year earlier), but the number of jobs did not grow.[@investomine]
- **The Middle East is still a risk for oil.** Attacks on ships continue even as oil prices ease, and the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage that much of the world’s oil sails through, remains the key pinch point.[@reuters-oil]

explain: **What “reports” means.** When a company reports, it publishes its sales and profits for the last three months. What moves the share price is not whether the results are good or bad, but how they compare with what analysts expected. Investors have already bought or sold based on those expectations, so strong results that everyone saw coming often barely move the price. The big jumps and falls come when a company does much better or much worse than analysts expected.
